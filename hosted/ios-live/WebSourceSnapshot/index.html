WHO-5 Well-Being Index

Not enough data

How is this scored?

Rate each statement from 0 to 5 for the past two weeks. Add the five answers (0–25), then multiply by four for the official 0–100 WHO-5 score. Higher numbers indicate higher self-reported well-being. All five answers are required.

In the app’s snapshot, the score divided by 100 supplies one of three equally weighted comparisons: Exercise, Mental health, Sleep. The A–E bands are app-defined: A = 100; B = 75–99; C = 50–74; D = above 0 below 50; E = 0. They are not WHO categories, medical severity or a diagnosis. An unanswered or older-than-14-day check-in is unknown.

The WHO-5 uses a two-week recall period. It should not be treated as a daily mood test. For daily journaling, optional energy and stress check-ins can earn logging XP without changing the WHO-5 score. A score cannot explain causes or replace a professional assessment. BPM remains physiological context and is not secretly converted into a mental-health or overall score.

CURIOSITY, WITH CONTEXT

Behind the science.

Exercise, mental well-being and sleep

Exercise uses age-appropriate WHO activity references; sleep uses CDC age-specific duration references. The WHO-5 is a standardized five-item mental well-being instrument with a two-week recall period and a 0–100 score.

The app averages the known Exercise, WHO-5 score/100, and Sleep comparison ratios, each capped at 1, when at least two are known. A ≥1; B ≥0.75; C ≥0.5; D above zero below 0.5; E = explicitly entered zero. Missing data are excluded. These letter bands and the combined snapshot are app-defined, not WHO diagnostic categories or a validated health score.

BPM is retained as physiological context, without a hidden contribution to the grade. A single pulse value cannot establish health, fitness or mental well-being. No biological age, lifespan or disease probability is predicted.

Planned-route estimates

Minutes per kilometer are time per distance: a larger number means slower travel. The main calorie figure is active energy, excluding resting energy. At a fixed distance and gait, the ACSM equation’s horizontal cost is largely independent of pace. Total energy also includes 3.5 mL oxygen/kg/min of resting cost, so a longer outing can have a higher total.

Active oxygen cost = (0.2 × meters + 0.9 × ascent meters) for running, or (0.1 × meters + 1.8 × ascent meters) for walking. Multiply by body mass in kg ÷ 1000 × 5 kcal/L oxygen. These are population-based estimates, not measurements. Downhill is conservatively treated as level ground; very steep or unsupported paces do not receive a calorie number. Running estimates use 3–7.5 min/km and walking estimates use 10–20 min/km.

Elevation is a terrain estimate from the Copernicus 90-meter grid, not surveyed pavement altitude. The same rounded coordinate reuses the same source height; distance-based samples are at most 50 m apart, but denser sampling does not improve the underlying 90 m resolution. Planned profiles use those heights without direction-dependent smoothing. Ascent and descent are summed separately; on an exact closed return path they balance. Bridges, tunnels, trees, buildings, steps, and map errors can still cause disagreement with real height.

Routes use Valhalla pedestrian routing, reject ferry maneuvers, and reject drawn points farther than 30 m from the returned network. If routing is unavailable, unchecked sketches do not receive route estimates. This does not certify legal access or physical safety.
